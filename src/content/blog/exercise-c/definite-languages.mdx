The finite-language result is part of a broader connection with **definite languages**.

## Definition

A language $L\subseteq\Sigma^+$ is definite if it can be written as

$$
L=E\cup\Sigma^*F,
$$

where $E$ and $F$ are finite languages. Membership is therefore determined by finitely many exceptions and, for all sufficiently long words, by a bounded suffix.

The language $\Sigma^*ab$ is definite: take $E=\varnothing$ and $F=\{ab\}$.

## Algebraic characterization

A classical characterization states:

> A regular language is definite if and only if every idempotent in its syntactic semigroup is right-zero.

Combining this characterization with the right-zero criterion immediately gives:

## Corollary

If $L$ is definite and $S=(S_f,S_\omega)$ is constructed from its syntactic semigroup, then

$$
|S_\omega|=1.
$$

In particular, for every nonempty word $w\in\Sigma^+$,

$$
L=\Sigma^*w
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|S_\omega|=1.
$$

## Relation to the worked example

For $L=\Sigma^*ab$, the idempotents are $a$, $ab$, and $bb$. The Cayley table shows that each of them is right-zero. The direct linked-pair calculation and the definite-language theorem therefore give the same result:

$$
S_\omega=\{[a,a]\}.
$$

The theorem is more useful than the example alone because it proves the result uniformly for every suffix language $\Sigma^*w$, without recomputing its semigroup and conjugacy classes from scratch.

## Scope of the result

Right-zero idempotents are a sufficient condition for ω-triviality and characterize definite languages at the finite-word level. The converse statement

$$
|S_\omega|=1
\Longrightarrow
\text{every idempotent is right-zero}
$$

is not established here. The SCC question in the next article explores whether ω-triviality may arise from other structural mechanisms.
